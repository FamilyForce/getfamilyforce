Why it matters

Most parents miss critical windows — not because they don't care. Because they're exhausted.

The first three years of a child's life contain hundreds of time-sensitive developmental windows. Some are open for months. Others close in weeks.

The research is clear: timing matters. Acting in the right window dramatically improves outcomes. Acting outside it — or not at all — can mean missed opportunities that don't come back.

Most parents don't find out about these windows until after they've closed. Not because they weren't trying — because no one told them in time.

🥜
⚠️ Real example — Window closes
Peanut introduction
(months 4–6)
The research is unambiguous: introducing peanuts between 4 and 6 months reduces the risk of peanut allergy by up to 80%. The window is short. After 12 months, the benefit largely disappears.

Most parents find out about this window after it closes — from a friend, a blog post, a moment of panic. Scout tells you before it matters.
Source: LEAP trial (Learning Early About Peanut allergy), published in NEJM, 2015. The most cited allergy study of the last decade.

About Jack

He got it wrong with First Son.
Got it right with Second Son.

Jack Hartley is a dad from Dallas. When his first son was born, he did what most parents do: Googled frantically, took advice from relatives, and trusted that the important things would somehow surface on their own.

They didn't. The peanut window closed. A language milestone was flagged at a routine checkup — months after the ideal window. Nothing catastrophic. But avoidable.

"I didn't build Scout because I'm an expert. I built it because I'm a dad who learned too late what the research already knew. I don't want other parents to find out the same way I did."

FamilyForce is Jack's effort to make the clinical research that pediatricians rely on accessible to every parent — not in a textbook, not in a $400 consultation, but in your inbox once a month, before the window closes.

Every playbook, every window, every email in Scout is written in Jack's voice: a dad who's read the research so you don't have to.

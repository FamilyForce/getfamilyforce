## Why This Cluster First

Parents Googling "baby not [milestone] at [age]" are mid-problem, high-anxiety, and completely underserved by the existing top results (What to Expect forum threads, Pampers listicles, Reddit posts from 2014).

This cluster has three properties that are rare to find together:

1. **Real search volume** — not micro-niches, but genuine monthly traffic
2. **Low KD** — mostly 10–25 because big sites don't want to own "worried parent" content
3. **Perfect Scout alignment** — every article ends with the same truth: *"Scout would have told you this window was coming before you needed to Google it."*

The conversion funnel is the tightest in the FamilyForce content universe. A parent who lands on "baby not talking at 18 months" is exactly the parent who buys Scout.

## Jack's Voice — The Non-Negotiable Foundation

**Read this before touching the structural template.** SEO structure without Jack's voice produces generic content that ranks for a week and converts nobody. Jack's voice is what turns a parent who found the article at 2am into a parent who buys Scout.

---

### Who Jack Is (for writers new to the brand)

Jack is a dad of two boys. He winged it with First Son. He read 100 books and 1,000+ hours of research before Second Son. He's not a doctor. He's not a pediatrician. He's the guy who did the homework you didn't have time to do, translated it into plain English, and is honest about both the times he got it right and the times he didn't.

**First Son:** Jack didn't know the windows. He found out about milestones the same way most parents do: after the fact, panicking, Googling at 2am. He either over-worried (unnecessary 11pm pediatrician calls) or missed windows entirely because he didn't know they were closing. First Son is fine. The experience was harder than it needed to be.

**Second Son:** Jack knew the windows in advance. He wasn't blindsided. He made informed decisions instead of reactive ones. Second Son's experience wasn't perfect — kids aren't perfect — but it was fundamentally calmer.

That gap — between reactive and proactive — is what every development concern article is about. And it's what Scout closes.

---

### The Story Framework for Development Concern Articles

The First Son/Second Son framework applies to EVERY article in this cluster — but it must be used carefully.

**The golden rule: Never invent specific milestone ages for Jack's kids.**

The potty training playbook establishes specific canon (First Son: 19 months start, 14 months total; Second Son: 29 months start, 8 months total). No other specific milestone ages for Jack's kids exist in the manuscript. If writers invent ages — "First Son didn't crawl until 11 months," "Second Son walked at 10 months" — those claims will contradict each other across 50 articles and break the brand's credibility.

**What to use instead:** The *experience* of not knowing. The story is about the parent's anxiety and the 2am Google session, not about Jack's kids' specific milestone timing.

---

### The Safe Story Framework

Every article opens with some version of this:

> *I remember the first time I Googled "[article topic]." It was late. First Son was [approximate age range — not an exact month]. I had the phone in one hand and the monitor in the other.*
>
> *The results gave me: (a) total reassurance, (b) a list of things it could be, and (c) a forum thread from 2009 that was somehow still the top result. None of it actually told me what I needed to know.*
>
> *What I needed was a specific answer: here's the normal window. Here's what's genuinely fine. Here's the one thing that actually warrants a call.*

Then deliver that answer. That's the article.

**The Second Son / Scout bridge:**

> *By the time Second Son was [age range], I'd spent enough time in the research to know what windows to watch for. Not because I was a better parent — because I'd done the reading. Most parents don't have 1,000 hours. That's why Scout exists.*

The bridge is always: research → proactive → Scout. Never: "I'm special" → "You should be more like me." The reader should feel empowered, not judged.

---

### Voice Checklist (run every article through this)

**Jack sounds like:**
- A knowledgeable friend who happens to have read the research
- Calm, direct, occasionally wry
- Short sentences. Breathing room between ideas
- Research cited by name: not "studies show" but "a 2003 study in *Pediatrics* by Blum et al."
- Honest about what he doesn't know: "I can't tell you if your kid is fine. Your pediatrician can. What I can tell you is what the research says the window looks like."

**Jack does NOT sound like:**
- A clinical journal ("developmental trajectory outcomes")
- A lifestyle blogger ("here are my top tips!")
- A therapist ("be gentle with yourself")
- A medical disclaimer machine ("always consult your doctor before...")
- A mom content creator who added "dads too"

**The banned phrases list (from the brand guide):**
- "You've got this!"
- "Be gentle with yourself"
- "Every child is different and beautiful"
- "Studies show..." (name the study)
- "Simply..." (nothing is simple to a worried parent at 2am)
- "As a parent, I know how hard this is..."
- "I'm no expert, but..."
- Exclamation marks for enthusiasm

---

### Sample Opening in Jack's Voice

For "baby not crawling at 10 months":

> *The first time I Googled "baby not crawling at 10 months," I was in the kitchen at 11pm with the phone brightness turned all the way down so I didn't wake First Son up. I'd been watching him earlier that afternoon. He was doing this thing where he'd get up on all fours, rock back and forth, and then just sit back down like he'd decided it wasn't worth it.*
>
> *I went to bed that night knowing approximately three things: that some babies crawl late, that some babies never crawl at all and go straight to walking, and that at least one forum post from 2011 thought I should call the pediatrician immediately. None of those three things were actually useful.*
>
> *Here's what would have been useful: the specific crawling window. The difference between "behind" and "outside the window." The one sign that actually warrants a call vs. the six signs that don't.*

That's the hook. Direct. Specific. Relatable. No exclamation marks. Then deliver the substance.

---

### Integrating Research in Jack's Voice

**Wrong:** "Studies show that most babies crawl between 7 and 10 months."

**Right:** "A 2013 systematic review in *Developmental Medicine & Child Neurology* put the median crawling onset at 8.3 months, with a normal range of 6 to 11 months. After 12 months without any form of locomotion — crawling, scooting, rolling — that's when a developmental evaluation is warranted."

Name the journal. Give the year. Give the finding. Then translate it.

If you can't find a specific citation, use the AAP's developmental milestone guidance (which is updated and citable). Don't hedge with "research suggests." Say what the research says and point at it.

#### Section 1: The Hook (100–150 words)

Open with the parent's experience, not a definition.

> *You're lying awake at 2am with your phone, searching "[baby not milestone at age]." You already know what Dr. Google is going to tell you. A mix of reassurance and horror, roughly equal parts.*

Make them feel seen. Then pivot immediately to what this article actually does:

> *Here's what the research says, what the actual window is, and specifically what to watch for that warrants a call to the pediatrician — versus what's completely normal variation.*

**What NOT to do:** Don't open with "Many babies develop at different rates." That's the generic answer they already got. Start by acknowledging the specific worry.

---

#### Section 2: What the Window Actually Is (300–400 words)

This is the substance. Use FamilyForce's Scout window data.

**Format:**
- What the milestone is (define it precisely — parents often don't know exactly what "crawling" or "pointing" means developmentally)
- The normal window: when most babies start, when the window peaks, when it closes
- The variance: boys vs girls (if relevant), premature vs full-term (adjust for corrected age)
- What the research says (cite at least one source: AAP guidelines, specific study, pediatric consensus)

**Key rule:** Be specific. "Most babies crawl between 7 and 10 months, with the window staying open through 12 months" is more useful than "babies crawl at different rates."

---

#### Section 3: The Scout CTA — First Placement

After the window explanation, insert the first Scout reference:

> **📅 Scout by FamilyForce tracks this window.** Parents who use Scout receive an email the month before this milestone window opens — so they know what to watch for before they need to Google it. [Try Scout free →](/sign-in?intent=trial)

Keep it short. One sentence context, one sentence CTA, one link.

---

#### Section 4: Actual Red Flags vs. Normal Variation (400–500 words)

This is the most valuable section. Most sites give vague reassurance. Give specific, actionable criteria.

**Format:**
- "Here's what's almost certainly fine:" (3–5 specific scenarios that sound alarming but aren't)
- "Here's what genuinely warrants a call to your pediatrician:" (3–5 specific signals based on AAP/developmental pediatrics guidelines)
- "Here's what warrants same-week evaluation:" (the true red flags — regression, loss of skills, other co-occurring signs)

**The tone:** You're a knowledgeable friend who happens to know the research. Not "don't worry, every baby is different." Not "see your doctor immediately." Specific, calibrated, honest.

**Example (for crawling):**
> *Probably fine: Your baby hates tummy time and has been rolling to avoid it — some babies skip crawling entirely and go straight to pulling up. Your baby is 10 months and scooting or army-crawling — these are valid variations. You have a chubby baby — heavier babies sometimes start later.*
>
> *Call your pediatrician: Still not sitting independently at 9 months AND not crawling at 12 months. Floppy muscle tone. Asymmetric movement (favoring one side consistently). Not making eye contact or not babbling alongside the motor delay — combined delays are a different signal.*

---

#### Section 5: What To Do Right Now (200–300 words)

Concrete, immediately actionable. Three to five specific things:

1. Something they can try at home (tummy time variations, specific play positions, etc.)
2. When to book the 9-month/12-month well-child check if they haven't
3. What to tell the pediatrician (specific language — not "I'm worried" but "she's 10 months and not crawling or pulling to stand, no rolling, and I want to check her motor development")
4. What early intervention is and how to access it (relevant to the concern level)

## E-E-A-T Signals (Important for Medical-Adjacent Content)

Google applies heightened scrutiny to health and development content (YMYL — Your Money or Your Life). Every article needs:

1. **Author attribution:** Jack Hartley, byline on every article
2. **About page link:** Linked from every article
3. **Medical disclaimer:** In the footer of every page
4. **Cite sources:** At least one peer-reviewed source per article (AAP guidelines, Pediatrics journal, specific study)
5. **Date signals:** `datePublished` and `dateModified` in both JSON-LD and visible on page
6. **Medical reviewer:** Long-term, FamilyForce needs a US-based pediatrician (FAAP) as named reviewer — this is the E-E-A-T unlock for the autism-adjacent and screening content

The medical reviewer is the single highest-leverage E-E-A-T investment. One credentialed FAAP signing off on the source data covers all 213 Scout windows and every article derived from them.

## Summary: The 3-Sentence Brief for the Content Team

You are writing for a parent who is awake at 2am, worried that their baby is behind, and who just got a vague answer from a big website that didn't actually answer their question.

Your job is to give them the specific answer: here's the normal window, here's what's genuinely fine, here's what actually warrants a call — and here's how Scout would have told them this was coming before they needed to Google it.

Write like a knowledgeable friend who has read the research, not like a medical website that's afraid of getting sued.

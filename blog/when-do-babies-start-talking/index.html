When Do Babies Start Talking?
The Complete Month-by-Month Guide

By Jack Hartley · March 31, 2026 · 10 min read

Most parents are watching for first words. But language starts months earlier — in coos, in babbles, in the moment a baby turns toward your voice. Here's the full timeline, the science behind it, and the red flags pediatricians actually watch for at each stage.

Your baby is already communicating. From the first cry in the delivery room, they're producing intentional signals. The question isn't when language starts — it's what it looks like at each stage and what to do when it isn't tracking.

Language development is one of the most studied areas in pediatric research, and the findings are consistent: the timeline is predictable, the warning signs are well-established, and early intervention works. Here's the complete picture.

The Language Development Timeline

Birth – 4 Weeks
Crying, startling, calming to voice

Crying is communication. Your newborn has already learned your voice — they heard it in utero for months. A one-month-old will often stop crying at the sound of a familiar parent before being picked up. That recognition is real language groundwork.

6 – 8 Weeks
Cooing — the first intentional sounds

The social smile arrives around 6 weeks, and with it, the first coos — soft, drawn-out vowel sounds (ohhh, ahhh). These are not random. Your baby is experimenting with their voice intentionally and beginning the serve-and-return exchange that language is built on. When your baby coos: coo back. That back-and-forth is building neural architecture.

⚠️ No cooing by 2 months → raise at 2-month visit
4 – 6 Months
Babbling begins

Babbling — repeated consonant-vowel combinations like "ba-ba," "da-da," "ma-ma" — bridges cooing and words. It should be emerging around 4 months and well established by 6. Research shows that babbling frequency and variety at 6 months predicts vocabulary size at 18 months. What you do now matters later. Respond to every babble. Use infant-directed speech (short, clear, slightly higher-pitched sentences — what used to be called "baby talk"). It accelerates language acquisition.

⚠️ No babbling by 6 months → raise at 6-month visit
9 Months
Pointing and joint attention

Around 9 months, babies begin pointing at things to share interest — then looking back at you to see your reaction. This is called declarative pointing, and it's one of the most important language milestones of the first year. Babies who point more at 9–12 months have larger vocabularies at 18 months. Absent pointing by 12 months is the single strongest behavioral predictor of autism spectrum disorder. When your baby points, look at what they're pointing at, name it, and respond with enthusiasm. "Yes! A dog. Big dog."

⚠️ No pointing by 12 months → raise immediately
10 – 14 Months
First words

Most babies say "mama" and "dada" as sounds around 9–10 months. By 12 months, these should be used specifically — "mama" for mom, "dada" for dad — not as general babble. First true words beyond mama/dada typically arrive between 11 and 14 months. "Ball," "dog," "more," "up" — these count. Sign language counts. A consistent approximation — "bah" always for ball, "dah" always for dog — counts as a word. The bar is intentional, consistent use. Not pronunciation perfection.

⚠️ No words at all by 15 months → raise at 15-month visit
15 – 18 Months
10 words → the vocabulary spurt

Ten words by 15–18 months is the standard developmental checkpoint. And it's not just a number — it's a threshold. Research shows a clear vocabulary acceleration, the "vocabulary spurt," that typically begins after the 10-word milestone. Once it starts, children may add new words daily, sometimes 9–10 per day. Getting to 10 words is what triggers it. The engine runs differently after 10 words than before. This is why early language delays compound: missing the 10-word threshold delays the spurt that was supposed to follow it.

⚠️ Fewer than 10 words by 18 months → raise at 18-month M-CHAT visit
18 – 24 Months
50 words + two-word combinations

Fifty words is the gate that opens the next stage. Once a child reaches approximately 50 words, two things happen: vocabulary explodes, and two-word combinations begin. "More milk." "Daddy go." "Big dog." These aren't just extra words — they're constructed meaning. Your child is no longer labeling; they're talking. The 50-word milestone typically arrives between 18 and 24 months. By 24 months, research suggests most children have approximately 200 words, though the range is wide. What matters is that language is growing and two-word combinations are present.

⚠️ Fewer than 50 words at 24 months → raise at 2-year visit ⚠️ No two-word combinations at 24 months → raise at 2-year visit
24 – 30 Months
Three-word sentences

"I want milk." "Daddy come here." "No more nap." Three-word combinations mark the transition from telegraphic speech to early grammar. Your child is constructing sentences with structure — not just content words, but relationships between them. Three-word sentences typically emerge between 24 and 30 months and are the primary language checkpoint at the 30-month well-child visit, which the AAP added specifically for developmental surveillance. Grammar errors at this stage are normal and expected. "I goed to the park" is a sign of language development, not a mistake to fix.

⚠️ No three-word sentences by 30 months → raise at 30-month visit
30 – 36 Months
Full sentences + 75% intelligibility

By 36 months, most children speak in sentences of 4 or more words and can be understood by unfamiliar adults approximately 75% of the time. Language is now a reliable, versatile communication tool — not just labels and requests, but narrative, explanation, and emotional expression. "I went to the park and I saw a dog and it was really big and I ran away." That's a 3-year-old. Grammar is still developing — irregular verbs, irregular plurals — but sentences are real.

⚠️ Mostly 2-word phrases at 36 months → raise at 3-year visit

The Single Biggest Driver of Language Development

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls serve and return "the most important thing parents can do for brain development." The concept is simple: your baby makes a sound, a gesture, or a facial expression — the "serve." You respond: make eye contact, mimic, talk back, engage — the "return." Every exchange builds neural connections. Millions of these exchanges in the first years wire the architecture for language, emotional regulation, and learning.

What it looks like in practice: when your baby coos, coo back. When they babble, babble back. When they point at a dog, say "dog! big dog!" When they say "ball," you say "red ball" or "roll the ball." You're not running drills. You're having a conversation — one where your job is to follow their lead and respond with slightly more than they gave you.

The expansion technique

When your child says a word, expand it. If they say "dog," you say "big dog" or "brown dog running." If they say "milk," you say "more milk" or "cold milk." Research shows expansion accelerates vocabulary faster than drilling new words or explicitly teaching. The child provides the topic; you extend it. This is the most effective language-building technique available and it costs nothing except attention.

What Doesn't Build Language

Screens. The research on screens and language development is consistent: passive screen exposure does not build vocabulary. It displaces the back-and-forth conversation that does. A baby watching educational videos is not getting serve-and-return. A baby babbling at you while you make breakfast is. The AAP recommends no screen time for children under 18 months except video calls. For 18–24 months: limited, high-quality content, always watched together.

Talking at your baby instead of with them. Narrating ("now we're putting on your shoes") is valuable — but only if it includes pauses for response. One-way monologue builds familiarity with language. Back-and-forth builds the neural circuits for using it. Both matter. The ratio matters more than the total volume of words.

The Red Flag Checklist — What Pediatricians Watch For

These are the flags that warrant a conversation at your next well-child visit — or sooner if you're concerned:

  • No startling to loud sounds or calming to voice by 1 month → hearing concern, don't wait
  • No cooing by 2 months → raise at 2-month visit
  • No babbling by 6 months → raise at 6-month visit
  • No pointing by 12 months → raise immediately (primary autism screening flag)
  • No first words by 15 months → raise at 15-month visit
  • Fewer than 10 words by 18 months → raise at 18-month M-CHAT visit
  • Fewer than 50 words at 24 months → raise at 2-year visit
  • No two-word combinations at 24 months → raise at 2-year visit
  • No three-word sentences by 30 months → raise at 30-month visit
  • Mostly two-word phrases at 36 months → raise at 3-year visit
  • Strangers can't understand most of what they say at 36 months → request speech eval

On waiting to see if it resolves

The most common mistake parents make with language delays is waiting. "He'll catch up." "Her brother was a late talker." Maybe. But early speech evaluation and intervention — before 18 months if possible, definitely before 24 months — has strong evidence of effectiveness. Waiting costs time that matters. If you're uncertain, the evaluation itself answers the question. There's no downside to asking.

Boys vs. Girls — The Language Gap Is Real but Overstated

Boys do tend to develop language slightly later than girls, on average. This is well-documented in the research. But "slightly later on average" does not mean "the red flags don't apply to boys." The milestones above represent meaningful thresholds for all children. A boy with no words at 15 months needs the same evaluation as a girl in the same situation. Don't let the boys-talk-later generalization become a reason to delay raising a real concern.

Reading Aloud — The Simplest Thing with the Biggest Return

The AAP recommends reading aloud starting at birth. Children who are read to daily have significantly larger vocabularies, better literacy skills, and stronger cognitive development by school age. The research is unambiguous and the intervention is free.

Your baby does not understand the words. Read anyway. The sound of language, the rhythm of sentences, and the physical closeness all matter before comprehension arrives. The habit formed in month one is the habit that persists. Any book counts. Board books, picture books, whatever you have. The content matters less than the consistency.

Frequently Asked Questions

When do babies start talking?

Babies begin communicating from birth. Intentional cooing starts around 6–8 weeks, babbling around 4–6 months, first words around 12 months, two-word combinations around 18–24 months, and full sentences by 30–36 months.

What is the first word a baby usually says?

Most babies say "mama" or "dada" first, though by 12 months these should be used specifically for mom and dad rather than as general babble. First meaningful words beyond mama/dada typically appear between 11 and 14 months — "ball," "dog," "more," "up."

Is my baby a late talker?

The key flags: no babbling by 6 months, no pointing by 12 months, no first words by 15 months, fewer than 10 words by 18 months, fewer than 50 words or no two-word combinations by 24 months, no three-word sentences by 30 months. If any apply, raise it at your next well-child visit. Early speech evaluation is highly effective — waiting costs time that matters.

How can I help my baby talk faster?

Talk constantly during daily activities and pause to let your baby respond. Use serve-and-return: respond to every coo, babble, and gesture. Name objects in context. Read aloud from birth. Use expansion — when your child says "dog," respond with "big dog" or "dog running." Reduce passive screen time: it displaces the conversation that builds vocabulary. The single biggest driver is back-and-forth human interaction. You're the intervention.

What is the vocabulary spurt?

The vocabulary spurt is a period of rapid vocabulary growth that typically begins after a child reaches 10 words — usually around 15–18 months. Once it starts, new words may appear daily. The spurt is triggered by reaching the 10-word milestone, which is why early language delays compound and why the 10-word checkpoint at 15–18 months is so important.

Do boys talk later than girls?

Boys do tend to develop language slightly later than girls on average, and this is well-documented in the research. But the developmental red flags apply to all children regardless of sex. A boy with no words at 15 months needs the same evaluation as a girl in the same situation. "Boys talk later" is not a reason to delay flagging a real concern.

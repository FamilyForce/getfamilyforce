4 Month Sleep Regression — What It Is, Why It Happens, and How to Get Through It

By Jack Hartley ·March 31, 2026 ·8 min read

Your baby slept a 4-hour stretch at 10 weeks. Now at 4 months they're up every 45 minutes and you're wondering what you did wrong. You didn't do anything wrong. Their brain just changed. Here's what's actually happening — and what to do about it.

What the 4-Month Sleep Regression Actually Is

It's called a "regression" but that's the wrong word. Your baby hasn't gone backward. Their brain has gone forward — and sleep has gotten harder as a result.

Here's what changed: newborns cycle between light sleep and deep sleep in a relatively simple pattern, and they can often transition between cycles without fully waking. Around 3–5 months, the brain reorganizes its sleep architecture to resemble adult sleep — with distinct stages (light sleep, deep sleep, REM) and brief natural awakenings between each cycle.

Those cycles last roughly 45 minutes. When a baby surfaces between cycles at the end of each one, they do what any of us would do if we woke up in unfamiliar conditions: they check whether everything is the same as when they fell asleep. If it isn't — if they fell asleep being rocked or nursed but now find themselves in a crib — they alert.

This is why the 4-month regression specifically targets families who rock, nurse, or hold their baby to sleep. The sleep association — the condition required for falling asleep — is the mechanism. The baby wakes between cycles, finds the association gone, and cries for it to come back. Every 45 minutes. All night.

The key distinction

Other sleep regressions (8 months, 12 months, 18 months) are temporary — caused by developmental leaps that pass. The 4-month regression is different: the underlying neurological change is permanent. Newborn sleep architecture doesn't come back. This is why addressing sleep associations during the 4-month window matters more than waiting it out.

Signs You're in the 4-Month Sleep Regression

  • A baby who was sleeping 3–5 hour stretches is now waking every 45–90 minutes
  • Longer wake windows before sleep than before — harder to settle
  • Naps are capping at exactly 45 minutes (one sleep cycle) then waking fully
  • More fussiness and overstimulation during the day
  • Needing more help getting back to sleep than the first stretch of the night required
  • Starts around 3.5–4.5 months — occasionally as early as 3 months

Why It Happens at 4 Months

The timing isn't random. Around 3–4 months, several things happen simultaneously in the developing brain:

  • Sleep architecture matures — the brain transitions from simple newborn sleep cycling to the multi-stage sleep architecture that humans maintain for life. This is neurological progress, not a problem.
  • Cortisol regulation develops — the stress hormone system starts becoming more responsive. This makes babies more reactive to stimulation and harder to settle when overtired.
  • Circadian rhythm solidifies — the light/dark cycle starts influencing sleep more strongly. The 4-month period is often when a more predictable day/night pattern first becomes possible — but only if sleep associations aren't getting in the way.
  • Motor development is accelerating — rolling is beginning or imminent, tummy time is intensifying, and the brain is processing enormous new motor input. This neurological activity competes with sleep pressure.

The Swaddle Warning

The 4-month regression often coincides with when rolling begins. This creates an urgent safety issue: a swaddled baby who rolls to their stomach cannot push themselves back up. This is a suffocation risk — not a theoretical one.

The AAP is unambiguous: stop swaddling the moment your baby shows any signs of rolling, attempting to roll, rocking side to side, or getting one shoulder off the mat during tummy time. Most babies show these signs between 2 and 4 months.

⚠️ Stop swaddling tonight if any rolling signs are present

This is not a gradual transition. The day you see a rolling attempt — stop swaddling that night, cold turkey. Yes, sleep will likely get worse for a few nights. That's the cost of the safety. It's worth it. Transition to a sleep sack (arms out) immediately.

What Makes It Worse

Overtiredness. At 4 months, wake windows are typically 1.5–2 hours between sleeps. Going much longer than this before a nap or bedtime causes cortisol to spike — making it harder, not easier, to fall asleep. The overtired baby who fights sleep is experiencing an elevated cortisol response to being kept awake too long. Watch the clock, not just the tired cues.

Inconsistency. Different sleep situations (car, carrier, crib, arms) make it impossible for the baby to build a predictable expectation for what sleep looks like. Consistency matters most during the regression window because the brain is actively trying to build a sleep pattern.

Late bedtime. Many parents push bedtime later hoping the baby will sleep longer. The opposite is usually true. An early bedtime (7–8pm for most 4-month-olds) reduces overtiredness and often improves both night sleep and morning wake time.

What Actually Helps

1. Address the sleep association

The most effective long-term approach is teaching your baby to fall asleep independently — placed down drowsy but awake rather than fully asleep. This way, when they surface between sleep cycles at 3am, they can connect them on their own without needing you. Every evidence-based sleep approach (Ferber, Weissbluth, gentle methods) is built on this foundation. The method is less important than the consistency.

2. Consistent bedtime routine

A short, predictable sequence — bath, feed, song, dark room, put down — signals to the developing circadian system that sleep is coming. Keep it under 30 minutes. Do it in the same order every night. The routine is a neurological cue, not just a comfort ritual. Within 1–2 weeks of consistency, most babies show a measurable improvement in sleep onset.

3. Optimize the sleep environment

Dark: blackout-dark. Light suppresses melatonin. A completely dark room makes a meaningful difference in both sleep onset and early morning waking.

White noise: continuous, consistent sound (not nature sounds with variation) masks the household sounds that cause light-sleep-phase waking. Place it between the baby and the door, not in the crib.

Cool: room temperature comfortable for a lightly dressed adult. Overheating is a SIDS risk and also disrupts sleep quality.

4. Watch wake windows

At 4 months: 1.5–2 hours awake between sleeps. At 5 months: creeping toward 2–2.5 hours. Most 4-month-olds need 3–4 naps per day — this will consolidate to 3, then 2, over the next several months. Respecting the wake window prevents the cortisol spike that makes sleep onset harder. If your baby is fighting a nap, they're likely overtired — not under-tired.

What not to do

Don't wait it out indefinitely. The underlying sleep architecture change doesn't reverse. Waiting for it to "pass like other regressions" can turn 6 weeks of disruption into 6 months. The window where sleep habits are easiest to establish is 4–6 months — before stronger separation anxiety and object permanence make changes harder.

Don't introduce new sleep associations to survive the regression. Nursing or rocking to sleep more during the regression digs the hole deeper. If anything, this is the time to work toward independent sleep onset — not away from it.

How Long Does It Last?

The neurological change is permanent — newborn sleep architecture doesn't return. But the disrupted nights themselves typically last 2–6 weeks. The difference between a family that's through it in 3 weeks versus one that's still struggling at 6 months comes down almost entirely to whether sleep associations were addressed during the window.

Families who address sleep associations at 4 months typically see improvement within 1–2 weeks of consistent effort. Families who don't often find that the 4-month disruption blends seamlessly into the 8-month regression, then the 12-month, then the 18-month — each one harder than the last because the habits are more entrenched.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 4-month sleep regression?

A permanent change in baby's sleep architecture that happens around 3–5 months. The brain matures from simple newborn sleep cycling to adult-like sleep stages with brief awakenings between cycles. Babies with sleep associations (needing to be rocked or nursed to sleep) wake fully between cycles and cry to recreate the conditions they need — often every 45 minutes.

How long does the 4-month sleep regression last?

The disrupted nights typically last 2–6 weeks. The underlying change is permanent. Families who address sleep associations during this window usually see improvement in 1–2 weeks. Those who don't can find the disruption continuing for months.

What are the signs of the 4-month sleep regression?

Waking every 45–90 minutes after previously sleeping longer stretches; naps capping at exactly 45 minutes; harder to settle at the start of the night; more fussiness during the day; needing more help getting back to sleep. Timing: usually 3.5–4.5 months.

How do I survive the 4-month sleep regression?

Short-term: optimize the sleep environment (blackout dark, white noise, appropriate room temperature), watch wake windows (1.5–2 hours at 4 months), and establish a consistent bedtime routine. Long-term: address sleep associations by placing baby down drowsy but awake so they can connect sleep cycles independently.

Is the 4-month sleep regression permanent?

The sleep architecture change is permanent — baby's brain won't revert to newborn sleep patterns. But the disrupted nights are not permanent. Once a baby can fall asleep independently, they can connect sleep cycles on their own and nights improve. The "regression" itself is the window to establish that skill.

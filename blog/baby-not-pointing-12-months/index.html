Baby Not Pointing at 12 Months? What the Research Says

By Jack Hartley·April 4, 2026·7 min read

Pointing is not a small thing. Declarative pointing by 12 months — pointing to share, not just to get — is the single strongest behavioral predictor of autism spectrum disorder in the first year of life. Here's what the research actually says, what pointing looks like, and what to do if it's absent.

I didn't know there were two kinds of pointing until Second Son was about eight months old and Scout sent us an email about it. The email explained the difference between pointing to get something and pointing to share something — and why only one of those matters for the 12-month developmental flag.

With First Son, I would have looked at him reaching toward his sippy cup and checked "pointing" off a mental list. I wouldn't have known I was looking at the wrong thing.

The distinction matters. Here's why.

Two Types of Pointing: Only One Is the Milestone

Imperative pointing — pointing to GET

The baby points at or reaches toward something they want. "Give me that toy." "I want that food." This is request-based. It's communicative, but it's about getting something. Imperative pointing develops earlier and involves the baby using the gesture instrumentally — as a tool to obtain what they want.

Declarative pointing — pointing to SHARE

The baby points at something to invite you to look at it too. "Look at that dog!" "Did you see that plane?" There's no request here. The baby isn't trying to get anything. They're sharing attention — inviting someone else into their experience of something interesting. This is the milestone. This is what the research measures. This is what's absent in many children later diagnosed with ASD.

The difference is motivational. Imperative pointing is about objects. Declarative pointing is about sharing experience with another person. It requires understanding that other people have perspectives worth sharing — a foundational element of social cognition.

The Research: Why This Milestone Carries so Much Weight

In 1992, Simon Baron-Cohen and colleagues published the CHAT (Checklist for Autism in Toddlers) study, which followed 18-month-old children and tracked which early behaviors predicted autism diagnosis. Absent declarative pointing emerged as the single strongest behavioral predictor — stronger than lack of pretend play, stronger than lack of eye contact, stronger than any single behavioral marker available at that age.

Multiple subsequent studies have replicated and strengthened this finding. Carpenter et al. (1998) documented the emergence of declarative pointing in typically developing infants and its relationship to joint attention more broadly. The M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up) — the primary autism screening tool used at 18-month and 24-month well-child visits — includes pointing as a primary item.

Scout missed-window guidance

No pointing by 12 months is one of the most significant developmental flags. Raise it immediately at the 12-month well-child visit. This is a primary screening point on the M-CHAT-R/F autism checklist. Don't wait for 18 months.

The Window

Joint attention and declarative pointing typically emerge between 9 and 11 months. In Scout's milestone data:

  • Window opens: ~7 months (Week 30)
  • Peak: ~8 months (Week 34)
  • Window closes: ~11 months (Week 47)

By 12 months, declarative pointing should be present. This is a closed-window situation — not "still inside the window." A 12-month-old who has never pointed declaratively is past the expected emergence window, and 12 months is the clinical flag point.

The Gesture Cluster

Pointing doesn't develop in isolation. It comes as part of a cluster of communicative gestures that emerge together and share an underlying mechanism: joint attention — the ability to coordinate attention between a person and an object or event.

The cluster includes:

  • Declarative pointing — extending the index finger to share attention
  • Showing objects — holding up an object to show someone, not to hand it over
  • Waving — hello, bye-bye
  • Following another person's point — when you point across the room, the baby looks where you're pointing

A baby who is absent on all of these behaviors at 12 months has a stronger combined picture than a baby who is missing only one. The absence of the full cluster — no pointing, no showing, no waving, not following your point — is clinically more significant than any single behavior in isolation.

How to test it at home

Point at something across the room — something you can see but the baby isn't already looking at. Say "look!" See whether your baby turns and looks where you're pointing. This is following a point — the receptive version of joint attention. Then watch whether your baby ever extends an index finger at something just to show you. Not to get it. Just to say "look at that." If neither behavior is present at 12 months, raise both at the well-child visit.

What to Do at the 12-Month Visit

The 12-month well-child visit is specifically structured to screen for early social and communication milestones. Pointing is on that list. If your baby isn't pointing declaratively:

  • Raise it explicitly. Say: "My baby doesn't point to share things — I've never seen them point at something just to show me."
  • Note the full picture: "They also don't wave" or "They don't follow my point when I point at something."
  • Ask about the M-CHAT-R/F screening and whether a developmental pediatrician referral is appropriate.

Early intervention for social communication concerns is available under IDEA for children under 3 — and like speech therapy, it doesn't require a diagnosis to begin. The evaluation determines eligibility.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should babies start pointing?

Declarative pointing typically emerges between 9 and 11 months and should be present by 12 months. The joint attention and pointing window in Scout's data opens at around 7 months, peaks at 8 months, and closes at 11 months. By 12 months, pointing should be present — not still emerging. A 12-month-old who has never pointed declaratively is at the clinical threshold and should be raised at the well-child visit.

Is not pointing at 12 months a sign of autism?

Absent declarative pointing at 12 months is the single strongest behavioral predictor of ASD in the first year, based on Baron-Cohen's CHAT study and replications. That doesn't mean every baby who isn't pointing will be diagnosed with autism — many won't. But it does mean the absence is a primary M-CHAT-R/F screening point and warrants immediate discussion at the 12-month well-child visit. Not watchful waiting. Not "let's check again at 18 months." Raise it now.

What's the difference between imperative and declarative pointing?

Imperative pointing is pointing to GET something — a request gesture. Declarative pointing is pointing to SHARE something — to invite someone to look at something interesting with you. There's no request involved. Declarative pointing requires that the baby understands other people have perspectives worth sharing, which is a key element of social cognition. Only declarative pointing is the autism screening signal. Imperative pointing alone is not the milestone.

My baby reaches for things but doesn't point — is that the same?

No. Reaching to request is an imperative gesture — it's about getting something. Declarative pointing uses an extended index finger specifically to share attention, not to request. Also check whether your baby follows your point: if you point across the room and say "look," does your baby look where you're pointing? A baby who neither produces a declarative point nor follows a point has a stronger combined picture — both producing and following points are part of joint attention development.

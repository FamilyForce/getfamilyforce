Baby Not Rolling Over at 4 Months? Here's the Actual Window

By Jack Hartley·April 4, 2026·6 min read

The tummy-to-back roll opens at 2 months and closes at 5 months. At 4 months, you're still inside the window for both rolling milestones. Here's what that actually means, why the windows are different, and the one thing that actually helps.

When First Son was about four months old, I was watching a video of someone else's baby rolling over and felt that specific low-grade parental anxiety: wait, is my baby supposed to be doing that? He was not rolling. He was doing a lot of staring at the ceiling and making satisfied noises, but rolling was not happening.

I Googled it at an unreasonable hour and found answers ranging from "by 3 months" to "by 6 months" with no explanation of which was right or why. It turns out both are partially right — because there are two rolling milestones, they're different, and most articles don't distinguish between them.

Here's the actual window.

Two Rolling Milestones (Not One)

The rolling milestone that most people picture is actually two separate skills with two separate windows, and they're often conflated in a way that creates unnecessary anxiety:

Tummy-to-back rolling (the easier direction)

Rolling from stomach to back. This is mechanically easier — gravity helps once the baby shifts their weight enough. This is the first rolling direction most babies master.

  • Window opens: 2 months (Week 8)
  • Peak: 3.2 months (Week 14)
  • Window closes: 5 months (Week 21)
  • Flag: not rolling tummy-to-back by 5 months → mention at next visit

Back-to-tummy rolling (the harder direction)

Rolling from back to stomach. This requires more core and shoulder strength and takes longer to develop. It comes after tummy-to-back rolling for most babies.

  • Window opens: 2.8 months (Week 12)
  • Peak: 4 months (Week 17)
  • Window closes: 6 months (Week 26)
  • Flag: not rolling in either direction at all by 6 months

At 4 months: you're still inside both windows

The tummy-to-back window closes at 5 months. The back-to-tummy window closes at 6 months. At 4 months, a baby who hasn't rolled in either direction is still within the normal range for both milestones. This is not a concern at 4 months. The flag is 5 months for tummy-to-back, and 6 months for any rolling at all.

The Tummy Time Connection

The most common reason babies roll on the later end of the window isn't a developmental problem — it's limited tummy time practice. Tummy time is the context where rolling practice happens. It's where babies build the neck, shoulder, and core strength that eventually produces a roll. Babies who get more tummy time generally roll earlier. Not because more tummy time fixes something that's wrong, but because tummy time is the practice environment for the thing.

If your 4-month-old isn't rolling yet and also hasn't had much tummy time, the prescription is straightforward: more tummy time, starting now, in short supervised sessions several times a day. Aim for hard surfaces or low-pile carpet rather than soft surfaces, which are harder to push against.

If your baby hates tummy time on the floor (extremely common), try it on your chest or across your lap. The position is what matters — not the surface.

The "Did It Once and Forgot" Phenomenon

One thing Scout covers in the rolling email that I wish I'd known with First Son: some babies roll once — complete surprise, everybody cheers — and then don't do it again for two weeks. Parents sometimes interpret this as regression or worry that something is wrong. It's not. Development isn't a straight line. The first roll is often accidental in a mechanical sense — the baby discovered the physics. Doing it reliably and intentionally comes later.

If your baby rolled once and hasn't done it since, that still counts as evidence of the emerging skill. Watch for it to become more consistent over the next few weeks.

Red Flags That Change the Picture

At 4 months, no rolling is not a concern. But certain patterns alongside the absence of rolling warrant attention:

  • Not rolling in either direction AND not pushing up on arms during tummy time by 5–6 months. A baby who isn't rolling and also isn't showing any upper body strength during tummy time — not pushing up, not lifting the head — is a different picture from a baby who is strong and active during tummy time but just hasn't made the complete roll yet.
  • Consistent asymmetric rolling. Always rolling to the left, never to the right — or vice versa — across multiple sessions and weeks. Some babies favor one side, but consistent inability to roll both directions can indicate a one-sided issue worth mentioning.
  • Noticeably low muscle tone. A baby who feels floppy during handling, doesn't push back against your hands during tummy time, or can't bear any weight on their arms during floor time alongside not rolling warrants a conversation with the pediatrician.

The actual flags: not before 4 months

Not rolling tummy-to-back by 5 months: mention at the next well-child visit.
Not rolling in any direction at all by 6 months: raise this with the pediatrician. This is the clinical threshold, not the 4-month mark.

What to Do Right Now

Increase tummy time frequency

Multiple short sessions throughout the day are more effective than one long session. Three to five minutes several times a day is a reasonable starting point. Build from there as your baby tolerates it. If the floor isn't working, your chest works. What matters is the position.

Add a side-lying toy

During tummy time, place an interesting toy slightly to one side — just out of reach. When your baby reaches for it and shifts their weight to one side, they're rehearsing the weight-shift pattern that becomes rolling. You're not training rolling; you're creating the conditions where it can happen.

Watch the windows, not just the milestone

The 5-month flag for tummy-to-back and the 6-month flag for any rolling are the actual action thresholds. At 4 months, the right answer is: you're inside the window, add tummy time, and check back at 5 months.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should babies roll over?

There are two rolling milestones. Tummy-to-back rolling has a window of 2 to 5 months, peaking at about 3.2 months. Back-to-tummy rolling has a window of about 2.8 to 6 months, peaking at 4 months. The flags are: not rolling tummy-to-back by 5 months (mention at next visit), and not rolling in any direction at all by 6 months (raise with pediatrician).

Is it normal for a 4-month-old to not roll over?

Yes — at 4 months, both rolling windows are still open. The tummy-to-back window closes at 5 months and the back-to-tummy window closes at 6 months. A 4-month-old who hasn't rolled is still within the normal range for both. This is not a concern at 4 months. The clinical flags come at 5 and 6 months, not at 4.

How can I help my baby learn to roll over?

More tummy time is the most direct support. Tummy time is the practice context for rolling — it builds the neck, shoulder, and core strength that rolling requires. Aim for several short supervised sessions daily. Place an interesting toy slightly to one side to motivate weight-shifting. If your baby hates floor tummy time, try it on your chest or lap. If the roll happens once and doesn't happen again for a week or two, that's normal — the skill is developing and consistency takes time.

What if my baby only rolls one way?

Tummy-to-back typically comes first because it's mechanically easier — that's expected. Rolling both directions (tummy-to-back and back-to-tummy) at different times is normal. The concern is rolling consistently to only one side — always left but never right — which can indicate a one-sided issue worth mentioning to the pediatrician, especially if you see other asymmetric movement patterns.

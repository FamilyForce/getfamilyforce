15-Month-Old Not Walking? Here's What the Research Says

By Jack Hartley·April 4, 2026·7 min read

Most babies walk between 11 and 15 months — and 15 months is the outer edge of that window, not comfortably inside it. Here's the actual timeline, what independent walking means, and what to do if it hasn't happened yet.

I remember a dad from our playgroup whose son had been cruising along the couch for what felt like three months straight. His pediatrician kept saying "he's close." At 14 months, still not walking. At 15 months, still cruising. At the 15-month visit the pediatrician finally referred them for a physical therapy evaluation — and it turned out the kid had low muscle tone that had been subtly visible in how he moved the whole time.

He caught up. He was fine. But the family spent four months wondering if the "he's close" reassurance was accurate, when the conversation they needed was actually about the window having a specific edge.

Walking has a window. Here's where it sits and what it means when you're at the outer edge of it.

The Walking Window: 11 to 15 Months

Both the AAP and the WHO identify independent walking as a milestone that typically appears between 11 and 15 months, with a median around 12 months. The window looks like this in Scout's milestone data:

  • Window opens: ~11 months (Week 47)
  • Peak: ~13 months (Week 56)
  • Window closes: 15 months (Week 65)

What this means: at 15 months, a child who isn't walking independently is at the edge of the clinical window — not safely inside it. The 15-month well-child visit exists partly to assess this milestone specifically.

What counts as independent walking

Three or more steps in sequence, without holding onto anything or anyone. That's the bar. Cruising — walking while holding onto furniture, a push toy, or a person's hand — does not count as independent walking. Many parents understandably count confident cruising as "basically walking," but clinically it's a precursor, not the milestone.

The Steps That Come Before Walking

Walking doesn't appear out of nowhere. It follows a sequence of motor milestones, each of which matters and each of which has its own window:

Pulling to stand (~9–10 months)

Using furniture or a person to pull from sitting or crawling to upright position. This is the first real weight-bearing on legs. A child who hasn't pulled to stand at all by 12 months is already behind the walking sequence — raise it at the 12-month visit, not the 15-month one.

Cruising (~9–11 months)

Stepping sideways along furniture while holding on. This is where a lot of babies spend several months before letting go. Cruising is a legitimate stage — it's building the strength, balance, and motor confidence that independent walking requires.

First independent steps (~11–13 months)

Letting go and taking 1–3 steps. These are often lurching, wide-gait, and immediately followed by sitting down deliberately (or falling). This is the beginning. Most babies wobble through this phase for a few weeks before stringing together more steps.

Confident walking (~13–15 months)

More steps, better balance, starting to change direction. The gait is still wide and arms are often out for balance, but the child is clearly locomoting independently. This is the milestone as the AAP defines it.

What 15 Months Actually Means

Fifteen months is the outer edge — not the safe-inside-the-window answer. Some parents look up "15-month-old not walking" and find sources saying "walking can happen any time up to 18 months." That's not what the AAP or WHO say, and it's not what the developmental research supports. The clinical flag is 15 months, and the 15-month well-child visit is structured specifically to assess it.

Scout missed-window guidance

Not walking by 15 months: raise this with your pediatrician. They may recommend physical therapy referral or simply observation, but the conversation needs to happen. Don't wait for the 18-month visit if 15 months has passed without independent walking.

The Bottom Shuffler Exception

One legitimate nuance: bottom shufflers — babies who sit upright and propel themselves on their bottoms rather than crawling on hands and knees — consistently walk later than crawlers. Research shows bottom shufflers tend to walk 1–3 months later on average.

If your child was a bottom shuffler, their walking timeline will likely be on the later end of the window. This is documented and not concerning in itself. But 15 months is still the clinical flag regardless — it's the edge of the evidence-based window even accounting for the bottom-shuffler adjustment.

Red Flags That Change the Picture

Late walking alone — in an otherwise typically developing baby — is usually a timing variation. But certain patterns alongside late walking are more significant:

  • Asymmetric movement. Consistently favoring one side: one arm, one leg, one hip. Asymmetry in early motor development can indicate a one-sided neurological issue and warrants evaluation regardless of overall walking timeline.
  • Noticeably low muscle tone. A baby who feels floppy when held or placed in standing, who doesn't weight-bear well, or whose limbs feel soft and difficult to stabilize. Low tone (hypotonia) is common and often benign — but it should be evaluated in the context of late walking.
  • Not pulling to stand at all by 12 months. The walking sequence builds on itself. A child who skipped pulling to stand entirely, or who has never borne weight on their legs voluntarily, is not just "taking their time" with walking.
  • Other developmental areas also behind. A child who isn't walking AND has limited babbling, limited eye contact, or isn't responding to their name has a broader picture that warrants comprehensive evaluation.

What to Do Right Now

At the 15-month well-child visit

Raise walking explicitly. Don't assume the pediatrician will ask — they will, but having it clearly on the table as your concern makes sure it gets adequate attention. Ask: Is this within the normal window? Does this warrant a physical therapy evaluation? What would make you want to see us sooner than the 18-month visit?

If 15 months has passed

Call the pediatrician's office now rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit. Describe what you're seeing: what motor skills are present, what movement looks like, whether you see any asymmetry. This gives them enough information to decide whether an earlier visit or a referral is warranted.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should a baby walk?

The walking window is 11 to 15 months according to AAP and WHO guidelines, with a median around 12 months. Independent walking means 3+ steps in sequence without support — cruising along furniture doesn't count. By 15 months, the window has closed and absence of walking is a clinical flag that warrants discussion with the pediatrician.

Is it normal for a 15-month-old not to walk?

Fifteen months is the outer edge of the normal walking window. A 15-month-old who isn't walking independently should have this raised at the 15-month well-child visit — the AAP specifically assesses it there. Bottom shufflers tend to walk 1–3 months later on average, but 15 months is still the clinical threshold regardless. The pediatrician may recommend physical therapy, observation, or further evaluation depending on the full picture.

What should I do if my 15-month-old isn't walking?

Raise it at the 15-month well-child visit, or call ahead if the visit isn't imminent. Ask specifically whether a physical therapy evaluation is warranted and what would make the pediatrician want to see you sooner than the 18-month check. Don't wait for 18 months if 15 months has passed without independent walking.

Could not walking at 15 months mean something serious?

Most late walkers turn out to be timing variations with no underlying issue. But the evaluation is warranted because some cases involve low muscle tone, hip dysplasia, neurological factors, or other conditions that benefit from early identification. Specific red flags alongside late walking — asymmetric movement, not having pulled to stand by 12 months, noticeably floppy muscle tone — warrant more urgent evaluation. Late walking alone, in an otherwise typically developing baby, is usually the less urgent picture.

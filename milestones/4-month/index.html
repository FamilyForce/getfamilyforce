4 Month Milestones

4 Month Baby Milestones — The Sleep Regression Nobody Warned You About

By Jack Hartley · Updated March 31, 2026 · 8 min read

Around 3 to 4 months, your baby's sleep permanently reorganizes. Most parents aren't ready for it. Here's what's actually happening — and what to do about it — along with every other milestone opening this month.

Four months is the milestone parents talk about the least before the baby arrives and the most in the middle of the night afterward. The 4-month sleep regression blindsides nearly everyone. It's not a growth spurt. It's not a phase that goes away on its own. It's a permanent neurological change in how your baby sleeps — and understanding what's actually happening is the first step to surviving it.

Beyond sleep, month four is packed. Laughing. Rolling. Head control. The swaddle transition. Babbling starting to emerge. There's a lot happening, and the window for each one is open for a limited time.

The 4-Month Sleep Regression — What's Actually Happening

Around 3 to 4 months, your baby's sleep architecture permanently reorganizes. Before this change, newborns cycle through sleep differently than adults — they spend more time in deep sleep and transition between cycles more smoothly. After the 4-month mark, sleep cycles start to resemble adult patterns, with distinct light-sleep phases between cycles where the brain briefly rouses.

Before this change, many babies slept through these transitions without waking. After it, they don't — because they haven't yet learned to connect cycles independently. Every time your baby surfaces into light sleep (every 45–60 minutes), they may fully wake and need help returning to sleep.

⚠️ Important to know

Unlike most regressions, the 4-month change is permanent. Sleep architecture doesn't revert. The goal isn't to wait it out — it's to help your baby learn to fall back asleep independently between cycles. Parents who introduce sleep associations during this period (nursing or rocking to full sleep) often find the disruption extends for months.

The regression typically lasts 2–6 weeks. The disruption improves once babies develop the ability to connect sleep cycles on their own. Consistent bedtime routines matter here. When your baby is developmentally ready — usually around 4–6 months — some version of independent sleep learning tends to shorten the hard period significantly.

Motor Milestones at 4 Months

Tummy time: 20 minutes per day by now

The AAP recommendation is 20 minutes of tummy time per day by 4 months — not all at once, but spread throughout the day. If that number sounds ambitious, start with 5-minute sessions and work up. Tummy time builds the neck, shoulder, and back muscles your baby needs for every physical milestone that follows: rolling, sitting, crawling, standing.

Without adequate tummy time, motor development slows. It also allows positional flat head (plagiocephaly) to develop and worsen — which is almost entirely preventable in the first few months and significantly harder to address after 6 months.

Jack's note

Babies hate tummy time. This is normal. Discomfort does not mean harm. The goal is tolerance, not enjoyment. Start with short bursts. Lie face-to-face on the floor with them. Use a rolled blanket under the chest. Persist. With my first son I caved when he cried and started late. With my second I started at day three and was consistent. The difference in motor development was significant.

Head control

Steady head control by 4 months is one of the foundational gross motor milestones. It signals that the neck muscles and upper spine are developing correctly. It's also a prerequisite for solid food readiness — babies who can't hold their heads up aren't ready for solids. Your pediatrician will assess this directly at the 4-month well-child visit.

If your baby is not holding their head up by 4 months, bring it up at that visit. It doesn't mean something is definitively wrong, but it warrants professional assessment.

Rolling — and the swaddle transition

Rolling typically happens between 3 and 5 months, but the signs appear earlier. The moment your baby starts showing rolling signs during tummy time — rocking onto one shoulder, getting a knee under the body, attempting to roll — the swaddle comes off.

⚠️ Safety — act the same day

A swaddled baby who rolls face-down cannot push themselves back up or move their face away from the mattress. The AAP is unambiguous: stop swaddling the moment your baby shows any signs of rolling. Most babies show these signs between 2 and 4 months. Don't wait for a full roll. Don't do a gradual transition. Stop that night. Sleep will get worse before it gets better. The safety trade-off is non-negotiable.

If your baby is already rolling and still being swaddled, stop tonight.

Cognitive Milestones at 4 Months

  • Tracking objects across full visual field — Your baby's vision has improved significantly. They can now follow a moving object or face smoothly from side to side and up and down, across their complete field of view.
  • Recognizing familiar faces and voices — At 4 months, your baby clearly distinguishes caregivers from strangers and shows a visible preference. This is the foundation for the social smile that's been developing since 6–8 weeks.
  • Laughing — The first real laugh usually appears between 3 and 4 months, typically triggered by something unexpected: a funny sound, peek-a-boo, an exaggerated facial expression. By 6 months, laughing should be frequent and interactive.
  • Responding to name — Not fully established yet, but your baby is starting to register that the sound you make most often is associated with them. Full, consistent name recognition develops more solidly around 6–8 months.
  • Reaching for objects with intent — Hands are starting to become tools. Your baby is moving from accidental grabs to intentional reaches, especially for objects in their visual field.

Language Milestones at 4 Months

Babbling — repeating consonant-vowel combinations like "ba ba," "da da," "ma ma" — typically emerges around 4 months. It should be well established by 6 months. This isn't just cute. Babbling frequency and variety at this age predict vocabulary size at 18 months and beyond.

If your baby isn't babbling by 6 months, that's a language flag worth mentioning at the next visit. It's one of the most consistent early language red flags in the research.

At 4 months, your baby is also starting to engage in "conversational turns" — making a sound, pausing as if waiting for a response, then making another sound when you respond. This back-and-forth is the foundation of all future language. Match their sounds. Pause. Let them respond. This is the most important thing you can do for language development right now.

Nutritional Milestone — Breastfed Babies Only

If you're breastfeeding, ask your pediatrician about iron supplementation at the 4-month well-child visit. Breast milk is nearly perfect nutrition, but it's low in iron. Babies are born with iron stores that last about 4–6 months. After that, breast milk doesn't replenish them fast enough.

Iron deficiency in the first year affects brain development, cognitive function, and behavior — and it's one of the most preventable nutritional problems in infancy. The AAP recommendation is 1 mg/kg/day of liquid iron drops for breastfed infants, continuing until iron-rich solids are well established around 6–7 months. Formula-fed babies don't need this — iron-fortified formula covers it.

What to Bring Up at the 4-Month Well-Child Visit

  • Sleep changes — frequency of waking, whether sleep has gotten significantly worse
  • Tummy time progress and current daily minutes
  • Head control (your pediatrician will assess directly)
  • Iron supplementation if breastfeeding
  • Whether babbling has started
  • Any loss of previously acquired skills (always flag this immediately)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 4-month sleep regression?

The 4-month sleep regression is a permanent change in how your baby's sleep is organized. Around 3 to 4 months, sleep cycles reorganize to resemble adult sleep — with light-sleep phases between cycles where the brain briefly rouses. Before this change, many babies slept through these transitions. After it, they don't, because they haven't yet learned to fall back asleep independently. Unlike other regressions, this one doesn't revert — it's a developmental change, not a temporary blip.

How long does the 4-month sleep regression last?

The 4-month sleep regression typically lasts 2 to 6 weeks. The disruption improves once babies develop the ability to connect sleep cycles independently. Parents who introduce sleep associations during this period — nursing or rocking fully to sleep — may find the disruption persists longer. Consistent bedtime routines and, when developmentally ready, some form of independent sleep learning tend to shorten the difficult period.

When should swaddling stop?

Swaddling should stop the moment your baby shows any signs of rolling — rocking side to side, getting one shoulder off the mat during tummy time, or attempting to roll. Most babies show these signs between 2 and 4 months. A swaddled baby who rolls face-down cannot push themselves back up. The AAP is unambiguous: this is a suffocation risk. Stop swaddling that night, cold turkey. Sleep will worsen temporarily. The safety trade-off is not negotiable.

What should a 4-month-old be doing?

At 4 months, most babies can hold their head steady without support, push up on their forearms during tummy time, show early rolling signs, laugh out loud, track objects across their full visual field, and start babbling. They recognize familiar faces and voices and show clear preferences for caregivers. Not every baby hits all of these at exactly 4 months — a range of a few weeks is typical for each skill.

Why is my 4-month-old suddenly waking up more at night?

If your baby was sleeping longer stretches and has suddenly started waking every 1 to 2 hours around 3 to 4 months, you're almost certainly experiencing the 4-month sleep regression. This is not a feeding issue, not a growth spurt in isolation, and not a sign something is wrong. It's a developmental change in sleep architecture — your baby's sleep cycles now include light-sleep phases between cycles, and they're waking because they haven't yet learned to bridge those cycles independently.

How much tummy time should a 4-month-old have?

The AAP recommendation is at least 20 minutes of tummy time per day by 4 months — spread across the day in multiple shorter sessions, not all at once. Tummy time builds the muscles needed for rolling, sitting, crawling, and standing. It also prevents positional flat head. If your baby hasn't been doing consistent tummy time, start now with short sessions and build up.

20 Month Milestones

20 Months: Jumping Starts and the Language Spurt Peaks

By Jack Hartley ·Updated April 1, 2026 ·6 min read

At 20 months, the vocabulary spurt is in full swing, jumping attempts are starting, and tantrums are at or near peak intensity. Three separate developmental systems are running at full speed at the same time.

Month 20 is dense. Physically, jumping is starting — both feet leaving the ground simultaneously, which requires more coordination than running. Linguistically, the vocabulary spurt is happening or about to happen. And emotionally, the tantrum peak at 18–21 months means this is often the hardest stretch of toddlerhood for parents.

None of this is random. Language and motor and social development are all peaking in this window because the brain is undergoing rapid synaptic pruning — the process that strengthens the most-used neural connections and eliminates less-used ones. More input now = more that gets locked in.

Motor: Jumping Both Feet Off the Ground

The jumping window opens around 20 months. True jumping — both feet leaving the ground simultaneously — is a significant coordination achievement. It requires timing, balance, and the leg strength to generate upward force.

  • Jumping attempts — Often one foot leaves the ground first, but both leave eventually. The early attempts don't look like jumping yet. That's fine.
  • Kicking a ball — First kicks are large swings that often miss. The ability to approach a ball and attempt to kick it is the milestone, not the accuracy.
  • Throwing overhand — Increasingly deliberate. Still no accuracy. The throwing motion is developing the shoulder coordination that feeds into many later skills.
  • Running — more controlled — Better stopping, sharper turns. Falls are less frequent on flat surfaces.

Language: The Spurt Is Here

The vocabulary spurt — the period when words arrive rapidly, sometimes 5–10 new words per week — typically peaks around 20–21 months. If you've hit the 10-word gate and been labeling consistently, you may be watching the spurt happen in real time right now.

Two-word combinations are the next gate. Most children start producing two-word combinations — "more milk," "daddy go," "big dog" — around 18–21 months, typically after they've accumulated around 50 words. At 20 months, early two-word combinations are expected and should be encouraged.

  • 30–50+ words — The 50-word milestone is the target for the 18–24 month window. At 20 months, many toddlers are in the 30–50 range or already past it.
  • Two-word combinations appearing — "More cracker," "mommy up," "big truck." Short, specific, intentional. Count them.
  • Understanding growing faster than production — Your toddler understands 200+ words even if they're only saying 30. They're taking everything in. Keep narrating.
  • Naming pictures in books — When you point to a picture and ask "what's that?" — they should be attempting names for familiar objects.

⚠️ Fewer than 20 words at 20 months

Don't wait for the 24-month visit. Call your pediatrician. The CDC flags fewer than 50 words by 24 months and no two-word combinations by 24 months as concerns requiring evaluation. Getting an assessment at 20 months gives you four months of intervention time before that check. Earlier is better.

Tantrums: At or Near Peak

The 18–21 month window is when tantrums peak in frequency and intensity. At 20 months, this is not getting better yet — it gets better when language fills in the gap, which starts happening more reliably around 24 months.

The strategies that work at 20 months:

  • Prevention: Keep to consistent sleep and meal schedules. Most intense tantrums happen when tired or hungry. This is a known variable you can manage.
  • In the moment: Name the emotion briefly, stay physically calm, don't negotiate or explain. "You're upset. I'm right here." Then wait.
  • After: Brief reconnection. No lecture. Move on.
  • Choices: Offer two real options whenever possible. Reduces the frequency of meltdowns by keeping decision-making within current cognitive capacity.

With First Son, I tried reasoning with a 20-month-old having a tantrum on the floor of a grocery store. It did not work. With Second Son I knew: wait it out, stay warm, don't escalate. It still happened in public, but it ended faster.

What to Do Right Now

  1. Read together every day. Point to pictures and name them. Ask "what's that?" and wait for the attempt. Reading frequency at this age is one of the strongest predictors of vocabulary at age 5.
  2. Model two-word combinations. When you narrate, use short two-word strings: "big dog," "more water," "daddy home." Your toddler's current language stage shapes what they pick up — they need to hear the structure they're about to produce.
  3. Protect sleep and mealtimes. Tantrum frequency is tightly linked to tiredness and hunger. Consistent nap and meal timing is the best tantrum prevention tool available at this age.

Month 21 is when the 50-word milestone, two-word combinations, and tantrum peak all converge. The work you're doing right now is what makes that month navigable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What milestones should a 20-month-old be hitting?

Running confidently, jumping attempts, kicking a ball, 30–50+ words, early two-word combinations, following 1-step instructions consistently, pointing to pictures in books, naming familiar objects. Tantrum frequency is at or near peak — that's expected at this age, not a behavioral disorder.

When does the vocabulary spurt happen?

Typically around 18–21 months, after a child reaches approximately 10 words. The spurt is when word acquisition accelerates rapidly — sometimes 5–10 new words per week. It can feel sudden. If your 20-month-old hasn't had a spurt yet, check the word count. Fewer than 20 words at 20 months with no upward trend is a signal to discuss with your pediatrician.

My 20-month-old seems to understand everything but doesn't say much. Is that normal?

Receptive language (understanding) always develops ahead of expressive language (speaking). At 20 months, your toddler may understand several hundred words while only saying 30. That gap is expected. What matters is that both are growing. If understanding is strong but speaking is minimal and not growing, mention it to your pediatrician — it's sometimes a sign of hearing issues or other language-specific delays worth assessment.

Is it true tantrums get worse before they get better?

Yes. The 18–21 month window is the peak. They begin declining in frequency and intensity as language develops through 24–30 months — not because your toddler is suddenly better-behaved, but because language fills the gap between what they feel and what they can express. The tantrums don't disappear; they become shorter and more manageable. By 30 months, most parents report a noticeable improvement.

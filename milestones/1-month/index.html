1 Month Milestones

1 Month Baby Milestones — Your Newborn's First Four Weeks

By Jack Hartley ·Updated March 31, 2026 ·6 min read

The first month is about survival — yours and theirs. But underneath the exhaustion, a remarkable amount of neurological development is happening. Here's what to watch for and what it means.

What's Developing in the First Month

A one-month-old's brain is producing roughly 700 new neural connections per second. Most of it isn't visible yet — it's infrastructure. But some of it is: your newborn is already beginning to recognize your face, your voice, and your smell as distinct from the rest of the world. That recognition is real and it matters.

The milestones of the first month are modest compared to what comes later. The nervous system is still calibrating. Reflexes dominate over intentional movement. But the social brain is already online — earlier than most parents expect.

Motor Milestones at 1 Month

  • Lifting head briefly during tummy time — A few seconds, unsteadily. This is neck muscle development starting from scratch.
  • Turning head side to side — When on their back or stomach. Tracking your voice across the room.
  • Bringing hands to face — Not intentional yet, but they're finding their hands for the first time.
  • Strong grasp reflex — Will grip your finger tightly when you place it in their palm. This is a reflex, not intentional grasping — intentional reach develops around 3–4 months.

Start Tummy Time Now

Tummy time builds the neck, shoulder, and back muscles needed for every physical milestone that follows: rolling, sitting, crawling, standing. It also prevents positional flat head (plagiocephaly), which is almost entirely preventable in the first months and significantly harder to address after 6 months.

Start from day three. Even 30-second bursts count. Use your chest — lay baby face-down on your reclined chest. It counts as tummy time and they tolerate it better in the early weeks because they can hear your heartbeat.

They will hate it. Do it anyway. Discomfort is not harm. You're building muscle.

Tummy time target

The AAP recommends working toward 20 minutes of tummy time per day by 4 months — spread across the day, not all at once. Starting at 1 month gives you three months to build up. Starting at 3 months means you're behind. Start now.

Social and Communication at 1 Month

  • Startles to sudden loud sounds — The Moro reflex. Arms throw out, then pull in. This is the earliest hearing check you can do at home.
  • Calms to familiar voice — Your voice specifically. They learned it in utero. A one-month-old often stops crying when they hear mom or dad — that recognition is already there.
  • Brief eye contact — Holds your gaze for a few seconds. Visual focus is still limited to about 8–12 inches — the exact distance from your face during feeding.
  • First smiles — reflex, not social yet — The social smile (smiling in direct response to your face) appears around 6 weeks. Smiles in the first month are typically reflex. Look for the social smile at 6–8 weeks.

⚠️ No response to sounds at 1 month

If your newborn does not startle to sudden loud sounds and does not calm to your voice, raise it at the 2-month well-child visit — or call before then. The newborn hearing screen at hospital discharge should have been completed. If it wasn't, or if the result was a "refer," schedule an outpatient audiology follow-up immediately. Early intervention for hearing loss before 6 months produces dramatically better language outcomes.

Newborn Screenings — Confirm These Were Done

Before leaving the hospital, three screenings should have been completed:

  • Newborn hearing screen — Painless, 10 minutes. A "refer" result is common and usually resolves on repeat — it doesn't mean hearing loss, but follow-up within one month is required.
  • Newborn blood spot (heel prick) — Tests for 30+ metabolic, hormonal, and genetic conditions including PKU, hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, and sickle cell. Most are treatable when caught early. Devastating when missed.
  • Bilirubin check — Newborn jaundice affects ~60% of term babies. Most cases resolve in 1–2 weeks. Severe untreated jaundice can cause brain damage. Confirm bilirubin was checked before discharge and that follow-up was scheduled.

If any of these were missed at the hospital, don't wait for the 2-month visit. Call your pediatrician this week.

Feeding at 1 Month

Newborns feed 8–12 times per day — roughly every 2–3 hours, including at night. Cluster feeding (multiple feeds in quick succession in the evening) is normal, particularly in the first weeks. It's not a sign that something is wrong with your milk supply.

Weight gain is the most reliable indicator that feeding is going well. Most newborns lose 5–10% of birth weight in the first days, then regain it by 10–14 days. If your baby has not regained birth weight by 2 weeks, your pediatrician needs to know.

Frequently Asked Questions

What milestones should a 1-month-old be hitting?

Lifting head briefly during tummy time, startling to loud sounds, calming to familiar voice, brief eye contact, and bringing hands to face. The social smile (in response to your face) is coming — typically around 6 weeks.

When should tummy time start?

Day three. Even 30-second bursts from the beginning build neck muscle and prevent flat head. Laying baby face-down on your reclined chest counts. The AAP recommends working toward 20 minutes per day by 4 months — easier to reach if you start early.

When do newborns start smiling?

The social smile — smiling in direct response to your face and voice — typically appears around 6 weeks (late in month 1 to early month 2). Smiles before 6 weeks are usually reflex. No social smile by 3 months is a clinical flag to raise at the 3-month visit.

How often should a 1-month-old feed?

8–12 times per day, roughly every 2–3 hours including at night. Cluster feeding in the evenings is normal. Weight gain is the most reliable sign that feeding is going well — birth weight should be regained by 10–14 days.

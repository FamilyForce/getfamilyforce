⚠️ Closing this month

Car seat installation check — rear-facing, before birth/discharge

safety  ·  advisory

Car accidents are a leading cause of injury death for young children. Research shows up to 75% of car seats are installed incorrectly — an incorrectly installed seat provides substantially less protection.

The move

•Check that the seat doesn't move more than 1 inch when you tug the base

•Free inspections are available at many fire stations and hospitals — search "[your city] car seat inspection"

•If you haven't had it checked, do it this week
🏃

⚠️ Closing this month

Tummy time — start from birth

motor  ·  advisory

Tummy time builds the neck, shoulder, and back muscles your baby needs for every physical milestone that follows — rolling, sitting, crawling, standing. It also prevents positional flat head (plagiocephaly), which is almost entirely preventable in the first months and significantly harder to address after 6 months.

The move

•Start now if you haven't: 2–3 minutes, 2–3 times per day

•Use your chest — lay baby face down on your chest when you're reclined. It counts.

•Right after a diaper change is a natural moment to try
💬

⚠️ Closing this month · ~26 days left

Responds to sounds / startles

language  ·  clinical Clinical

From birth, a baby should startle to sudden loud sounds and calm to a familiar voice. These are the earliest signs that hearing is functional. Absent or inconsistent response to sound in the first weeks can be an early indicator of hearing loss, which affects 1–3 per 1,000 newborns. Early detection produces dramatically better language outcomes.

The move

•Confirm the newborn hearing screen was completed at the hospital — ask for the result if you're not sure

•At home: clap or make a sudden sound near (not directly at) the baby's ear and watch for a startle response

•Note whether your baby calms specifically to your voice

Social smile — spot it and return it

social  ·  clinical Clinical

The social smile, appearing around 6 weeks, is the first sign your baby is engaging with the world intentionally — not gas, but a real response to your face and voice. Absent social smiling by 3 months is a clinical red flag and an early screening indicator for autism spectrum disorder. When your baby smiles at you, your response is the other milestone.

The move

•Get close — babies can only focus clearly at 8–12 inches

•Smile, talk, and wait. Give them time to respond. The social smile takes a beat.

•When they smile at you: smile back, talk to them, lean in — make it a two-way exchange

•Put everything else down during face-to-face waking time. These minutes matter disproportionately.
🏃

Head control — holds steady when upright

motor  ·  clinical Clinical

Steady head control by 4 months is one of the foundational gross motor milestones. It signals that the neck muscles and upper spine are developing correctly. It's also a prerequisite for solid food readiness — babies who can't hold their heads up aren't ready for solids. Absent or significantly delayed head control is one of the first motor red flags pediatricians check for.

The move

•Tummy time is the main driver — the work happens there

•Hold baby in supported upright positions during waking hours: facing outward in your arms, in a baby carrier

•At the 4-month well-child visit, your pediatrician will assess this directly
❤️

Parent mental health — screen for postpartum depression

social  ·  screening Screening

Postpartum depression affects 1 in 5 mothers and 1 in 10 fathers. Untreated PPD disrupts attachment, language development, and emotional regulation in children — the science is consistent on this. This is a screening window, not a judgment. The question isn't whether you're a good parent. It's whether your brain chemistry is working against you.

The move

•The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is the standard tool — your OB or pediatrician should offer it at the 6-week visit

•If you're not screened: ask. Specifically: "Can I do the postpartum depression screen today?"

•Partners should also screen — paternal PPD is real, underdiagnosed, and treatable
🛡️

Back to sleep, every time — firm surface, no loose items

safety  ·  advisory

Back sleeping reduces SIDS risk by 50%. The "Back to Sleep" campaign cut SIDS rates by more than 50% within a decade. The rule is absolute until 6 months: always on the back, for every sleep, every nap, every person who cares for this baby. Many SIDS deaths happen at grandparents' houses with caregivers who think "tummy sleeping is fine, we all did it."

The move

•Tell every caregiver: the rule is back sleeping, every time, no exceptions

•Tummy sleeping is only safe during awake, supervised tummy time — not for sleep

•After 6 months when a baby can roll both ways independently, they can be left in whatever position they roll to
🛡️

Parent infant CPR training

safety  ·  advisory

In the event of a choking incident or respiratory emergency, the minutes before emergency services arrive are critical. Parents who have received hands-on CPR training respond more effectively and more calmly than those who only watched a video. The training takes 2 hours and can be done before the baby arrives — or any time in the first few months.

The move

•Take a hands-on infant CPR and first aid class — your hospital, Red Cross, or local fire station offers them

•Video-only learning is better than nothing but significantly less effective than hands-on practice

•Make sure any regular caregivers — grandparents, nanny — are also trained
💬

Serve and return — the foundation of language

language  ·  advisory

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls serve and return "the most important thing parents can do for brain development." Your baby makes a sound or gesture (the "serve") — you respond (the "return"). Every exchange builds neural connections. Millions of these in the first years wire the architecture for language, emotional regulation, and learning.

The move

•Narrate everything you do: "I'm changing your diaper now. Left leg first."

•When your baby coos, coo back. When they look at something, look at it too and name it.

•Put the phone down during feeding and face-to-face time — your face is the most interesting thing in their world right now
